2026-10-25. An independent check of whether each claim in `curriculum-build/SCORECARD-EVIDENCE-REPORT.md`
is supported by the source it cites. DOIs, titles, authors and years were not re-verified (that was
done already). The question here is only: does the source say what the report says it says?

## Method

1. Parsed every bracketed citation in sections 1 to 12 of the report into (sentence, source) pairs.
2. Headline = sections 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12; every pair carrying an effect size or percentage;
   and the first-sentence "Basis" citations of each construct in section 6.
3. Read abstracts from OpenAlex, Europe PMC, arXiv, Crossref, publisher meta tags and Semantic Scholar.
   Where OpenAlex returned the abstract of a different paper (bandura1977, wood1976, alfieri2011;
   Crossref confirms the DOIs and titles in the sources file are right) the pair is "can't access".
4. Verdicts: supported (verbatim quote under 25 words), partly supported / overstated, not supported,
   can't access (no abstract in any source reachable; full text not read).
5. One source is often cited for several sentences. The verdict is for the main sentence; the same
   source verdict is repeated for its other uses unless the second use differs (then it has its own row).

Limits: abstracts only, so a finding that sits only in the full text can show as "can't access" or
"partly". 63 pairs (59 sources) could not be checked; most are older papers and books with no public
abstract (listed near the end).

## What was wrong, and what changed in the report

Not supported (1):
- **smith2014** was cited as support for "recall tests beat recognition tests". Its abstract says
  "little or no advantages of answering short-answer or hybrid format questions over multiple-choice
  questions". The typed-answers sentence now says the evidence is mixed (kang2007 found the better
  format depended on feedback).

Overstated or misattributed, fixed in the text:
- Bottom line 1 cited correlational studies (kratzig2006, knoll2017, husmann2019) as failing "the
  crossover test". Split into the crossover designs and the correlational ones.
- newton2017 is a survey of academics, not a review of evidence; moved to the "belief" group.
- major2021: the lower-effect group was interest-linked or feedback/support/assessment personalisation,
  not "interest-only". Reworded.
- simonsmeier2022: "hardly how much you gain" left out the paper's own warning of a very wide spread.
- crede2017: "mostly conscientiousness" became "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness".
- tetzlaff2025: "weaker for younger students / humanities" became "less clear evidence".
- gasevic2017 and craig2020: self-report validity worded as the papers state it (small but significant
  links; useful for two factors but weak link to metacognitive behaviour).
- Confidence stability: mazancieux2020 says metacognitive efficiency is partly consistent across tasks;
  rouault2018 and rahnev2020 do not test stability; stankov2014 shows a small increment over accuracy,
  not over self-efficacy. Reworded.
- Over-confidence list: rhodes2008, kornell2007 and hartwig2012 are about misled predictions and study
  choices, not over-confidence; split out.
- lomas2013 was cited as basis for target difficulty but found easier games more engaging and the most
  engaging versions slowest for learning. Note added.
- wilson2007 and bradbury2016 were cited for "attention in long expositions" but both find little
  support for a 10 to 15 minute attention drop. Sentence now says so.
- smith1994 argues against the "persistent, interfering misconception" view. Shown as a counter-view.
- ackerman2009: longer tests raised felt fatigue, but mean performance did not fall. Reworded.
- Rates: brown2001 warns about the coverage of simple intervals; agresti1998 and wilson1927 are about
  intervals. The report said they justify "logit counting variance". It now says that choice rests on
  the simulation.
- spiegelhalter2005 is about funnel plots, not shrinkage. Reworded.
- jiang2024follow, zhou2023ifeval, qin2024: abstracts do not report a drop with constraint count.
  Added that no paper tests a cap of 6.
- wang2024copilot is AI help for human tutors, henkel2024 is a preliminary extra-instruction study, and
  lehmann2025's abstract could not be read (the 48% and 17% belong to bastani2025). pardos2024's "32%"
  is of problems, not hints. The AI-tutoring sentence was rewritten.
- doroudi2019 found over half of RL policies beat baselines and rafferty2016 found faster learning;
  neither says policies "often fail with real ones". The simulation paragraph was rewritten around
  kaser2024, doroudi2017 and the offline-checking advice.
- parsons2019 recommends reporting split-half reliability but sets no 0.6 cut-off. Marked as our own.
- barzagar2019 found a spacing effect only at six weeks. kulik1988: classroom studies favour immediate

## Rules whose basis is weak (app code not touched)

1. **Prefer typed answers for a rushing learner** (rush_tendency directive in adaptation). The cited
   evidence is mixed (smith2014 none, kang2007 depends on feedback). The report already said "not
   shown"; it now also calls this a weak basis. Treat as a hypothesis to test.
2. **Brief carries at most 6 directives.** The papers show instruction following gets worse as
   instructions pile up; none tests a cap near 6. It is a design choice.
3. **challenge_appetite (target difficulty).** The 85% rule (wilson2019) is derived for algorithms and
   neural networks, not people, and the one large human-facing game study (lomas2013) found engagement
   and learning pull apart. The directive is driven by the learner's own taps, so the risk is low, but
   the "harder extension helps" half has no direct human evidence.
4. **brevity_preference.** The attention-limit evidence is contested; the case now rests on coherence
   and segmenting meta-analyses, which hold for learners in general, not for this learner's taste.
5. **The split-half r >= 0.6 test and the 0.98 posterior.** Both are our own thresholds. parsons2019
   and gelman2012 support the ideas (report reliability; partial pooling) but not these numbers. They
   stand on the simulation and the real-data checks in section 11.
6. **Rate variance (logit counting).** Chosen because it worked in simulation; the interval papers cited
   do not recommend it.
7. **hint_dependency / help_seeking (build hints into the text).** aleven2016help found better help
   seeking did not raise learning; the report's "not shown" note covers it.
8. **self_efficacy low (make step one achievable).** The evidence is correlational; talsma2018 finds
   performance predicts later self-efficacy more than the reverse.
9. **misconception_persistence.** Contrasting the wrong idea is supported by refutation-text
   meta-analyses (schroeder2023, g = 0.41), but the persistence premise has a counter-view (smith1994).
10. **Frustration, persistence and momentum bases.** Several key sources (baker2010, dmello2012,
    dmello2014, beck2013 beyond a snippet, ventura2013) could not be read. Titles fit; unconfirmed.

The expertise-reversal rules (prior_knowledge, scaffolding_fade) held up: tetzlaff2025, kalyuga2003,
oreilly2007, salden2010 and kalyuga2004/2005 say what the report says, with the one softening above.
