# Four times faster than normal studying: research, baseline, design, numbers, limits

Branch `four-x-modes`, 8 Nov 2026. Owner's target: Fastest = 4x faster than normal studying with equal or higher understanding and retention; Balanced = 2x faster AND higher understanding and retention; Highest = most understanding and retention.

**No real learner is in this file.** SIMULATED = the numbers come from a model. SOURCE = read in the paper or its abstract, today. ASSUMED = a judgement. Nothing was spent on models or APIs.

## Plain answer

**4x is not reached for most learners, and I did not lower the bar.** On held-out learners (SIMULATED) the as-built Fastest mode is **2.5x** faster than a typical student on textbook-density material, **1.1x** on the dense handouts we have as fixtures, with equal or higher recall and understanding on average. Only about 1% of learners reach 4x and equal-or-higher at textbook density (0% on handouts). Balanced is **1.6x** on textbook material with higher recall and understanding at a month (9% reach 2x and higher) and does not reach 2x. Highest gives +32 points of recall and understanding at a month, at about 3x the time of normal study.

Fastest is 4.4x faster than a **diligent** student (who remembers 7 points more and understands 6 more, so it misses the bar there) and about the same as a **lazy** one (who reads once; Fastest takes the same time and recalls 8 more points).

The limit is simple arithmetic, explained under "What limits it". 4x means about 20 seconds a testable point at textbook density, and reading plus one checked question costs about 66. The full design (one question over two parts, three-option questions) reaches 3.1x. Material with 5 times more words per testable point reaches 4x (x4.1, 25% of learners), and 8x reaches x6.6.

Real proof comes only from the tester study, which now records transfer (understanding) as well as recall.

## Research (every number checked today, 8 Nov 2026)

What students really do:
- 84% list rereading as a strategy, 55% rank it first; 11% report recalling from memory; 40% flashcards, 43% practice problems. Karpicke, Butler and Roediger 2009, Memory 17(4), read in the paper.
- Students mass work into the day or two before an exam and are deadline-driven. Blasiman, Dunlosky and Rawson 2017 (abstract), Hartwig and Dunlosky 2012 (abstract; PMID 22083626).
- Study time: about 14 hours a week preparing (Babcock and Marks 2010, as reported; NSSE first-years 14.3 hours), about 12 hours and a third under 5 in Arum and Roksa 2011 (secondary). Assigned reading: a third of students had no course with over 40 pages a week (Arum and Roksa, secondary).
- Reading: 238 words a minute for adult non-fiction, 183 aloud (Brysbaert 2019, abstract). Reading can't be sped up much without losing comprehension (Rayner et al. 2016, abstract, PMID 26769745). Workload rates: Rice University estimator, 180 and 127 words a minute at the "understand" level with some and many new concepts (read on the page; the estimator states it is reading research plus assumptions).

What memory does:
- Restudy gave 81%, 54%, 42% at 5 minutes, 2 days, a week; a test instead gave 75%, 68%, 56% (Roediger and Karpicke 2006, read in full, PMID 16507066). Four 5-minute restudy periods reached 83% at 5 minutes and 40% at a week; three tests reached 71% and 61%.
- After a word is learned, dropping it from further tests took week-later recall from about 80% to about 35%; dropping it from further study changed nothing (Karpicke and Roediger 2008, PMID 18276894, numbers as reported in Karpicke et al. 2009).
- Retrieval over restudy: meta-analyses report about g 0.50 to 0.61 (Rowland 2014, abstract; Adesope et al. 2017, Crossref abstract; effect sizes as reported in secondary sources).
- Transfer: testing beats restudy at d 0.40 overall (95% CI 0.31 to 0.50), 192 effects; best to application and inference questions, helped by elaborated retrieval and initial success, weakest to untested material (Pan and Rickard 2018, read in the PDF abstract). Repeated tests beat repeated study on new inference questions at a week (Butler 2010, abstract, PMID 20804289).
- Successive relearning: practice to 3 correct recalls and relearn 3 times at wide spacing is efficient; initial criterion and relearning are subadditive (Rawson and Dunlosky 2011, abstract, PMID 21707204). In a real course, 10% higher on the exam and 40% higher nearly a month later than the students' own study (Rawson, Dunlosky and Sciartelli 2013, as reported by Rawson and Dunlosky 2022 and a secondary page, paper's own minutes not read).
- Spacing: the best gap grows with the test delay, from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week delay to 5 to 10% of a 1-year delay (Cepeda et al. 2008, abstract, PMID 19076480; Cepeda et al. 2006, abstract, PMID 16719566). Spacing, not massing, beats cramming even in small flashcard stacks (Kornell 2009, abstract).
- Long-term loss: students keep much of what they learn; forgetting curves for classroom knowledge decline less than lab curves (Semb and Ellis 1994, abstract). Doctors' unrehearsed basic science holds for 1.5 to 2 years and then decays (Custers and Ten Cate 2011, abstract, PMID 21401691).

What can be removed:
- College textbook chapters compared with summaries of their main points: summaries were at least as good, at 20 minutes, a week and 6 to 12 months, including inference and transfer tasks, even with the main points underlined (Reder and Anderson 1980, abstract; Reder and Anderson 1982 on why). This is the main evidence for showing only key statements.
- Removing extra material helps in short lab lessons (coherence principle, Mayer's review; a secondary chapter says 13 of 14 experiments, median effect near 1 and short controlled lessons mostly). Mayer et al. 1996 itself I could not read.
- Interleaving: g 0.42 overall, mostly visual categories; expository text ambiguous, words negative (Brunmair and Richter 2019, abstract). I did not use it to claim any time saving on textbooks.
- Tutoring and mastery: human tutoring 0.79, step-based tutors 0.76 (VanLehn 2011, abstract and author's slides); intelligent tutors median 0.66 over conventional instruction, weaker on standardised tests (Kulik and Fletcher 2016, secondary). Time: the OLI statistics course covered a 15-week semester in 8 weeks with equal or better exams (Lovett, Meyer and Thille 2008, read). Mastery programs 108 evaluations, effect not read (Kulik et al. 1990, abstract shown cut short). Worked examples and expertise reversal: only a bibliographic hit (Tetzlaff et al. 2025); not used for any number.

What stacks and what can't:
- Retrieval and spacing stack (their effects are on the same memory, but both raise it). Retrieval replaces rereading, it does not sit on top of it.
- Relearning sessions are subadditive with the initial criterion, so a stricter first pass followed by reviews doesn't add up.
- Removing material and retrieval stack only until the point removed is what the question asks. That's why the omission check exists.
- Reading speed-up does not exist as a lever. Prior-knowledge skipping saves only what is proven known, and proving costs a question each.

## Frozen baseline

`docs/four-x/baseline.json` and `docs/four-x/BASELINE.md`, commit 03e0dab, written before any results. Lazy (one read, half a skim before a known test), typical (read, one reread, notes, an eve-of-test reread), diligent (read, reread, reread on day 2, one self-test a point, eve reread). Class time is left out, which helps the normal student. Cross-checked against published hours and page counts.

## Model changes

`scripts/four-x-sim.js` (constants read from `baseline.json`; a run marks itself SIMULATED). Prior knowledge is a real memory trace with a strength and age. Memory uses FSRS-6's curve for everyone, strengths set from Roediger and Karpicke. Recall and understanding (transfer) are measured separately. Learners vary in speed, forgetting and learning rate; the schedule learns each learner's forgetting from their reviews (kept at the default for sparse-data learners).

## Design: the three modes

Everything Fastest and Balanced drop stays one tap away.

- **Fastest:** key statements only (about 35 words per part, the rest under "More from your material"), one question a part with a one-line why, reviews only as needed to hold 55% recall on a horizon up to 60 days (50% to 180 days, 40% to a year), one question a look.
- **Balanced:** same page, one question a part, an application question on every second part, reviews to hold 60%, 55%, 50%.
- **Highest:** the full lesson, a worked example for newcomers, practice to 3 right, application questions, reviews to hold 93%.

A test on a known date gets one last question on the eve for any part below 90%. No mode skips a part; "skip what is known" costs a question to prove and saved no time on the design seed. Omission check: `services/learning-modes.js` compares each question's answer against the key statements, and anything not on the page is added to it, so a question never asks what the page left out. That check is word-based and simple.

Held-out seeds used only for the final check. One fix after the first held-out look (seeds 20261141 and 20261142): a dated test lost 11 points of recall to the cramming student, so an eve look was added and everything re-run on fresh seeds 20261151 and 20261152. Those are the numbers below.

## What limits it

1. **The time floor per point is reading plus one question.** At textbook density a typical student spends about 22 minutes on the handouts we have (about 8 points). 4x means 5.5 minutes, about 42 seconds a point, reading and answering included; and that is with a right answer on the first try. Fastest uses about 65 seconds a point: a question costs about 33 seconds when right and about 75 when wrong (the one-line why plus a retry), on top of about 14 seconds of reading. Practice is 6.5 of its 8.7 minutes.
2. **On dense handouts normal study is already short** (about 90 seconds a point), so there is nothing to compress.
3. **Fastest can't take further points without losing the retention it has to match:** at a month it is only +3.3 / +0.7 over typical, so there is little slack to spend.
4. **Against a cramming student on a known date, retrieval alone struggles to win on the day.**
5. **The lever I most wanted is unbuilt:** the "one question over two parts" and three-option format (x3.0 in the simulation, no real evidence for a two-part item; Schneid et al. 2014, as reported in a search result, is the only source for 36 versus 41 seconds).

What would have to be true for 4x (SIMULATED what-ifs in `sens.txt`): material with 5 times the words per point (x4.1), or 8 times (x6.6), or questions at 15 seconds with 8-second feedback (x3.7, no source for such items). The honest finding: 4x is reachable only on material that is dilute relative to its testable content, for learners who study normally on it. Real tester data decides the actual multiple.

## What is built (LEARNING_MODES, default off)

Wired for uploads:
- `services/learning-modes.js`: modes, floors, schedule rule, lesson view, omission check.
- Mode choice on the retention goal (`services/retention-goal.js`, `routes/retention-goals.js`, `public/js/retention-goal.js`): `PUT /api/retention-goals` takes `mode`; the row shows a select.
- `services/material-study.js`: one practice question per part (Fastest, Balanced), one question a review look, the mode's review timing and stop rule through `material-review-expand.js`, the key-points-only page with the rest under "More from your material", Fastest's one-line why.
- Off, none of this runs and stored modes are ignored.

Not wired, said plainly:
- Curriculum: the mode is stored on an enrollment goal and listed, but no curriculum path, page or review reads it.
- Application questions and worked examples (Balanced, Highest) are in the simulation, not in the app.
- The app's review timing uses the half-life model, not FSRS, so the simulated schedule is the design, not the code's exact dates.
- Pair questions and three-option format: not built.
- `npm test` covers the logic (`scripts/four-x-test.js`); a real learner path through a Fastest lesson is unit-tested, not walked in a browser.

Study (`services/upload-study.js`, `UPLOAD_STUDY`): already compares each learner against their own normal study. Now one of the two test questions per part is a tier-3 (combine or transfer) question when the part has one, and transfer is scored apart (`transfer_savant`, `transfer_normal`, `analyse().transfer`). Browser check: `scripts/four-x-browser.js` (Playwright, phone width, real app with a scripted model) opened the mode picker on a real account and saved a mode, and rendered the study's offer, test card and test screen with study replies mocked at the placement boundary; screenshots were taken. The consent flow and normal-part pages run by unit test, not in a browser, and a real timed run has never happened.

Fact-check and safety gates unchanged. `lessonView` only shows what is already built and checked.

## Not verified

Everything on a real learner. Real seconds (per question, per reread). Real textbook word-per-point density (the x3 is an assumption; the fixtures are dense). What a key-points-only page teaches (Reder and Anderson supports summaries; the sensitivity row shows Fastest fails if a page teaches 30% less). The transfer gains in `baseline.json` are ASSUMED; only their order comes from sources. Karpicke and Blunt 2011 and Mayer 1996 could not be read in full.
